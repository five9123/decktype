You have decided to learn a new language, and someone told you that typing practice can help. But you are staring at a blank screen, not sure where to begin. Maybe you cannot even read the script yet. Maybe you installed a Korean keyboard but the keys do not match what you expected. Maybe you tried typing a Japanese word and got completely different characters than intended. This is normal. Every single person who learns to type in a new language goes through this initial confusion. The good news is that the confusion clears up fast, and the path from here is more straightforward than you think.

This guide walks you through everything you need to get started with typing-based language learning. We will cover keyboard setup, script basics, your first words, and how to use tools like [typee.app](https://typee.app) to build momentum without getting overwhelmed.

## Setting Up Your Keyboard

Before you type a single character, you need the right keyboard layout installed on your device. Every major operating system supports multiple language keyboards, but the setup process varies.

**For Korean (Hangul).** On macOS, go to System Settings, then Keyboard, then Input Sources, and add Korean. The standard layout is called "2-Set Korean" and maps Hangul consonants to the left hand and vowels to the right hand. On Windows, go to Settings, then Time and Language, then Language, and add Korean. On mobile devices, add Korean in your keyboard settings. The physical layout is intuitive once you learn the basic principle: consonants on the left, vowels on the right. You can switch between English and Korean input with a keyboard shortcut, typically Command-Space on Mac or Alt-Shift on Windows.

**For Japanese (Hiragana/Katakana).** Japanese input works differently from most languages. You type in romaji (Roman letters) and the system converts your input to hiragana automatically. Typing "ka" produces か, typing "shi" produces し. On macOS, add Japanese Romaji input. On Windows, add Japanese and select Microsoft IME. You will also need to learn how to convert hiragana to katakana and kanji using the space bar and enter key. Start by just typing hiragana — katakana and kanji conversion can wait.

**For Chinese (Pinyin).** Chinese input uses pinyin, the romanization system. You type the pinyin for a character and select from a list of matching characters. On macOS, add Simplified Chinese Pinyin input. On Windows, add Chinese and select Microsoft Pinyin. The conversion from pinyin to characters involves selecting the correct character from candidates, which requires some character knowledge. For absolute beginners, start by practicing pinyin typing itself before worrying about character selection.

**For Spanish and French.** These languages use the Latin alphabet with additional characters. On most systems, you can use the standard English keyboard with special character shortcuts. On macOS, holding a letter key shows accent options. On Windows, you can use the international keyboard layout or Alt codes. Alternatively, add a Spanish or French keyboard layout that places accented characters on dedicated keys.

## Starting with the Script

If your target language uses a different writing system, learning the script is your true first step. Typing can actually accelerate this process because it forces active engagement with each character.

**Hangul (Korean).** Hangul is one of the most logical writing systems ever designed. It consists of 14 basic consonants and 10 basic vowels that combine into syllable blocks. The beautiful thing about learning Hangul through typing is that you learn the components individually. When you type ㅎ then ㅏ then ㄴ, they combine into 한 on screen. This decomposition makes the structure of each syllable block transparent. Most learners can read Hangul within a few days of practice, and typing dramatically accelerates this because you must process each component separately.

**Hiragana and Katakana (Japanese).** Japanese has two phonetic scripts of 46 characters each, plus thousands of kanji. Start with hiragana only. Because Japanese input uses romaji, you already know the input method — you just need to learn what each hiragana character sounds like and looks like. Typing "a-i-u-e-o" and seeing あいうえお appear on screen creates a powerful visual-auditory-motor connection. After hiragana feels natural, move to katakana, which represents the same sounds with different characters.

**Pinyin (Chinese).** If you are a complete beginner in Chinese, start with pinyin itself. Practice typing pinyin with correct tone marks in your mind. The four tones are the foundation of Chinese pronunciation, and typing forces you to internalize the romanization of each character. Character recognition comes later. Initially, just getting comfortable with how Chinese words sound and are romanized is a significant accomplishment.

**Latin scripts with diacritics.** For languages like Spanish, French, or Vietnamese that use the Latin alphabet with modifications, the script itself is not a barrier. Your challenge is training your fingers to reach for accented characters naturally. Practice typing words with accents repeatedly until the finger movements become automatic.

## The First 50 Words Approach

Trying to learn everything at once is the fastest way to quit. Instead, focus your first week on just 50 words. These should be high-frequency, concrete words that you will encounter constantly.

**Choose practical vocabulary.** Your first 50 words should include numbers (1 through 10), basic greetings (hello, thank you, excuse me), common objects (water, food, house, person), simple verbs (to go, to eat, to see, to do), and essential adjectives (big, small, good, bad). These words appear in nearly every conversation and text, so mastering them creates a foundation for everything that follows.

**Use typee's demo deck.** On [typee.app](https://typee.app), the demo deck for each language is designed specifically for this purpose. It contains carefully selected beginner vocabulary organized in a learning-friendly order. Start here rather than trying to build your own list from scratch. The demo deck handles the difficult work of word selection and sequencing for you.

**One word at a time.** When a new word appears, look at it carefully. Notice its shape, its components, the number of characters. Then type it. Do not rush. Speed comes later. Right now, accuracy and familiarity matter. If you type it wrong, look at the correct version, note where you went wrong, and type it again. This slow, deliberate practice builds stronger memory traces than rapid-fire typing.

**Keep a simple record.** After each session, write down (on paper or in a notes app) the three words you found hardest. These are the words that need extra attention. The next time you practice, try typing those three words before starting your regular session. This self-directed review catches words that might otherwise slip through the cracks.

## Progressing from Words to Phrases

Once you can type your first 50 words with reasonable accuracy, it is time to start combining them. This is where language learning gets exciting because you begin producing actual meaning.

**Start with two-word combinations.** If you know the words for "big" and "house," type "big house" in your target language. If you know "I" and "go," type "I go." These minimal phrases are not grammatically complete in most languages, but they represent a crucial step: you are combining vocabulary into meaning.

**Add basic sentence patterns.** Every language has a small set of sentence structures that cover an enormous range of expression. In Korean, the basic pattern is Subject-Object-Verb. In Japanese, it is similar. In Chinese, Spanish, and French, the basic pattern is Subject-Verb-Object. Learn one pattern and practice typing sentences that follow it using your known vocabulary.

**Use phrase cards.** On typee, create or find decks that contain short phrases rather than isolated words. Typing "I eat food" as a unit trains your brain to produce words in sequence, which is fundamentally different from recalling them in isolation. Phrase-level practice also exposes you to grammar naturally. You learn that certain words change form when combined, without needing to memorize grammar rules explicitly.

## Not Overwhelming Yourself

The most dangerous moment in language learning is not the beginning — it is about two to three weeks in, when initial excitement fades and the mountain of things you do not know becomes visible. Here is how to prevent that moment from derailing you.

**Set a minimum, not a maximum.** Instead of committing to 30 minutes a day, commit to opening typee once. Just opening the app counts. On most days, once you open it, you will practice for a few minutes. On bad days, opening and closing is enough to maintain the habit. Read more about building sustainable habits in our guide to the [15-minute daily routine](/blog/daily-typing-routine).

**Ignore what you do not know.** When you encounter an unfamiliar word in the wild, resist the urge to look it up immediately and add it to your study list. You have 50 words to focus on. Everything else can wait. Adding new words before mastering existing ones dilutes your practice and creates an ever-growing review burden.

**Compare yourself to yesterday, not to fluent speakers.** If you could type zero words in Korean last week and you can type twelve this week, that is extraordinary progress. The fact that native speakers know 50,000 words is irrelevant. Your trajectory is what matters, and your trajectory is upward.

**Accept imperfection.** You will misspell words. You will confuse similar characters. You will type at the speed of a glacier. All of this is completely normal and, more importantly, it is exactly how learning works. Mistakes are not failures — they are the raw material your brain uses to build better models. Learn more about [why your typing mistakes actually help you learn](/blog/typing-mistakes-learning).

## Celebrating Small Wins

Research on motivation shows that progress is the single strongest driver of sustained effort. But progress in language learning can feel invisible unless you actively look for it. Build celebration into your practice.

**Track your first milestones.** The first time you type a word correctly without looking at a reference is a milestone. The first time you type a complete sentence is a milestone. The first time you type a word and realize you did not have to think about it — it just flowed from your fingers — is a major milestone. Notice these moments.

**Share your progress.** Tell someone you are learning a new language and show them a word you can type. The social reinforcement adds an external layer of motivation that complements your internal drive.

**Review your history.** After two weeks of practice, look back at your first session. You will be surprised by how far you have come. Words that seemed impossibly complex on day one are now routine. This backward comparison is one of the most motivating experiences in language learning.

The path from complete beginner to confident typer is shorter than you think. It requires patience for about two weeks, and then momentum takes over. The script becomes readable. The keyboard becomes familiar. The first 50 words become the foundation for 500, then 5,000. Every expert speaker started exactly where you are now. The only difference is that they started typing.
