You have built a solid vocabulary foundation. You can type dozens or even hundreds of words in your target language from memory. The beginner stage is behind you. But something has shifted. Progress that once felt rapid has slowed. The daily practice that used to excite you has become routine. You are in the intermediate plateau, and it is the most common place where language learners stall.

The solution is not more of the same practice. It is different practice. Intermediate learners need techniques that push beyond individual word recall into the territory of fluent production: sentences, paragraphs, dictation, free composition, and timed challenges. This article introduces advanced typing techniques that will break through your plateau and reignite progress.

## Sentence Typing and Dictation

Single-word practice builds vocabulary but does not build fluency. Fluency requires producing words in sequence, with correct grammar, in real time. Sentence typing bridges this gap.

**Structured sentence practice.** Create or find decks on [typee.app](https://typee.app) that contain full sentences rather than individual words. When you type a sentence, you exercise vocabulary recall, grammatical knowledge, and word-order intuition simultaneously. A sentence like "Yesterday I went to the store and bought three apples" in your target language requires verb conjugation, time markers, number agreement, and object vocabulary — all in one production task.

**Dictation exercises.** Find audio content in your target language — podcasts, YouTube videos, audiobook samples — and type what you hear. This is one of the most demanding and effective typing exercises because it tests listening comprehension and production simultaneously. Start with slow, clearly spoken content. Pause after each sentence. Type what you heard. Then compare with the transcript if one is available. Over time, increase the speed and decrease the pauses.

**Shadowing through typing.** Shadowing is a technique where you repeat speech almost simultaneously with the speaker. The typing version involves listening to content and typing along in real time, without pausing. This is extremely challenging and should only be attempted after significant practice with paused dictation. But the payoff is remarkable: it builds the kind of automatic processing that characterizes true fluency.

## Speed Challenges and Timed Practice

Once accuracy is solid, speed becomes the next frontier. Fast typing in a foreign language is a direct indicator of fluency because it demonstrates automatic retrieval — you no longer need to consciously search for each word.

**Timed vocabulary sprints.** Set a timer for two minutes and type as many words as you can from a review queue. Track your count session by session. This is not about sacrificing accuracy for speed — errors still count. It is about pushing your comfortable pace upward. A learner who types 15 words per minute accurately in their target language is meaningfully more fluent than one who types 8 words per minute.

**Progressive speed training.** Start a sentence practice session at your comfortable speed. After five minutes, deliberately try to type 10 percent faster. After another five minutes, try another 10 percent. When errors start increasing, pull back slightly. This progressive overload — borrowed from athletic training — pushes your ceiling upward over time.

**Character-per-minute tracking.** On typee, monitor your characters-per-minute or words-per-minute metrics across sessions. Plot them over weeks. You should see a gradual upward trend. If the trend flattens, it is time to try a new technique from this article or to adjust the difficulty of your practice material.

## Contextual Vocabulary with Themed Decks

Random vocabulary lists are useful for beginners but become less efficient for intermediate learners. At your level, vocabulary is most effectively learned in thematic clusters — groups of words that you would naturally encounter together.

**Create themed decks.** Build decks around specific topics: food and cooking vocabulary, travel and transportation, workplace communication, medical terminology, or academic vocabulary. When words are learned in context with related words, they form stronger associative networks in memory. Learning "doctor," "patient," "symptom," "diagnosis," and "prescription" together creates a web of meaning that reinforces each word.

**Profession-specific decks.** If you are learning a language for work, create a deck focused on your professional vocabulary. An engineer might build a deck with technical terms. A teacher might focus on classroom language. A developer might collect programming-related vocabulary. This targeted approach ensures your typing practice directly supports your real-world needs.

**Current events decks.** Read news articles in your target language and extract vocabulary you do not know. Create a deck from each article. This approach ensures your vocabulary stays current and relevant. It also exposes you to formal register, which is valuable for reading and writing fluency.

## Creating Custom Decks from Real Content

The most powerful intermediate technique is creating your own study material from authentic content. This ensures you learn vocabulary that matters to you and provides natural context.

**Extracting vocabulary from articles.** When you find an article in your target language, read through it and identify words you do not know but could realistically learn. Do not collect every unfamiliar word — focus on words that appear useful and learnable given your current level. Add these to a new deck with the sentence from the article as context.

**Using typee's URL extraction feature.** On [typee.app](https://typee.app), the URL extraction feature allows you to paste a web article URL and automatically generate vocabulary cards from its content. The system identifies words at your level, creates cards with context sentences from the article, and organizes them into a ready-to-study deck. This transforms any web article in your target language into personalized study material within seconds.

**Building decks from conversations.** After a conversation with a native speaker or language partner, note the words and phrases you struggled with. These are high-value vocabulary items because they represent gaps in your practical communication ability. Create a deck from these notes and practice typing them until they become automatic.

**Subtitle mining.** Watch shows or movies in your target language with target-language subtitles. When you encounter useful phrases, add them to a deck. This provides natural, colloquial language that textbooks often miss. The emotional context of the scene also aids memory encoding.

## Free Composition Typing

At the intermediate level, you should regularly practice typing without any prompt — pure free composition in your target language. This is the most challenging and most realistic typing exercise because it mirrors actual language use.

**Daily journal entries.** Spend five minutes each day typing a short journal entry in your target language. Write about your day, your thoughts, your plans. Do not worry about perfection. The goal is production fluency — getting words from your brain to the screen without extended pauses. Check your text afterward with a dictionary or grammar tool if you want, but the primary value is in the act of producing language freely.

**Timed free writing.** Set a timer for three minutes and type continuously in your target language without stopping. Do not backspace, do not correct errors, do not pause to think of the perfect word. If you cannot think of a word, type around it using words you do know. This exercise builds tolerance for imperfection and develops your ability to communicate even when your vocabulary is incomplete.

**Responding to prompts.** Use writing prompts to guide your free composition. Simple prompts work best: "Describe your favorite food," "Explain how to get to your school," "Tell a story about your childhood." These focused topics prevent the blank-page anxiety that can make free writing feel impossible.

## Combining Techniques for Maximum Impact

No single technique is sufficient on its own. The most effective intermediate practice combines multiple approaches within a session. Here is a sample 20-minute advanced session structure.

**Minutes 1 to 5: Vocabulary review.** Use the [5-5-5 method](/blog/daily-typing-routine) to warm up with your review queue. Focus on accuracy.

**Minutes 6 to 10: Themed deck practice.** Work through a themed deck related to content you consumed recently — a news article, podcast, or TV show. This connects your typing practice to real language use.

**Minutes 11 to 15: Sentence dictation.** Listen to a short audio clip and type what you hear. Check your accuracy against a transcript. Note persistent errors.

**Minutes 16 to 20: Free composition.** Write a short paragraph about any topic. Push for speed without sacrificing too much accuracy. This final block is your daily measure of production fluency.

This varied structure prevents boredom, addresses multiple skill dimensions, and provides clear progress markers across different exercise types. Adjust the time allocations based on your specific needs — if dictation is your weakest area, give it more time. If free composition feels comfortable, reduce it and add a speed challenge instead.

## When to Move Beyond Intermediate

You will know you are ready for advanced-level practice when several things happen simultaneously. Your typing speed in the target language approaches your speed in your native language. You can type complete sentences without pausing to recall individual words. Dictation at normal speech speed feels manageable. Free composition flows without extended hesitations. At that point, your typing practice merges with genuine language use — you are no longer practicing typing a language, you are simply using it. And that is the entire goal.
